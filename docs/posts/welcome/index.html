Why Do Hospital Infections Remain a Problem in Germany?

Why fewer infections in hospitals do not always mean less harm across a country

The hidden problem behind hospital infections

When we visit a hospital, we expect to get better, not develop another infection.

However, some patients acquire infections while receiving healthcare. These are known as healthcare-associated infections (HAIs). They can lead to longer hospital stays, serious illness and, in some cases, death.

Germany presents an interesting situation. Its hospitals had a relatively low estimated infection rate compared with the European Union and European Economic Area (EU/EEA). Yet the estimated number of infections across its population was relatively high.

How can both of these things be true?

A study by Zacher and colleagues (2019) helps explain why looking only at hospital infection rates may not tell the whole story.

Looking at the evidence

The study used European hospital survey data from 2011–2012 and statistical modelling to estimate the impact of five common healthcare-associated infections.

The researchers examined the number of infections, deaths attributed to these infections, and the amount of healthy life lost.

For Germany in 2011, their model estimated approximately 478,000 infections and 16,200 deaths attributed to these infections.

The study also estimated around 249,000 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). This measure combines years of life lost through early death and years spent living with illness or disability.

These are estimates, not exact counts, but they show why hospital infections remain an important public health issue.

Why the numbers tell different stories

There are two ways to look at the problem.

The first measures infections among hospital patients. The second considers estimated infections across the country’s entire population over one year.

The results tell different stories.

As shown in Figure 1, Germany had fewer estimated infections per 100 hospital patients than the EU/EEA average: 2.40 compared with 2.83.

However, when annual infections were measured across the population, Germany’s estimated rate was higher: 592.1 infections per 100,000 people compared with 467.9.

These two measures have different denominators and should not be compared directly as if they were the same rate.

One possible explanation is Germany’s relatively high use of hospital services. When more people are admitted to hospital, there may be more opportunities for infections to occur across the population, even when the infection rate among hospital patients is relatively low.

This is why a low hospital infection rate does not necessarily mean a low national burden.

What should we learn from these findings?

The main lesson is that hospital performance cannot be assessed using a single statistic.

A relatively low infection rate among hospital patients is encouraging, but it does not automatically mean that the total impact across the country is small.

Hospital use also matters. A country with more hospital admissions may experience a greater number of infection episodes across its population.

For healthcare decision-makers, this suggests the importance of continuing infection prevention and considering ways to reduce avoidable hospital stays.

For the general public, the message is simpler: a positive-looking hospital statistic may not tell the complete story.

An important limitation

These results are based on statistical models using hospital data from 2011–2012. They should not be interpreted as Germany’s current infection burden.

The analysis included five common types of infection rather than all healthcare-associated infections.

The estimates also contain uncertainty. In particular, although Germany’s estimated burden of healthy life years lost was slightly higher than the EU/EEA average, the uncertainty was too large to establish a clear difference.

Finally, the study suggests that hospital use may help explain the findings, but it does not prove that reducing hospital admissions would produce a particular reduction in infections.

Conclusion

Germany’s experience shows why healthcare statistics need context.

Although its estimated infection rate among hospital patients was relatively low, the annual estimated infection burden across its population was still substantial.

The difference becomes easier to understand when we consider how frequently hospital services are used and what each measure actually counts.

The key takeaway is that we should look beyond a single hospital infection rate when evaluating public health outcomes.

Understanding both hospital-level rates and the wider population impact can support more informed discussions about healthcare quality and infection prevention.
